# What Drives Finnish (FI) Electricity Prices: Short-Term and 6-Month Outlook (as of 1 November 2026)

Over the next few days to weeks, the Finnish spot price depends mostly on wind output, in combination with temperature and the availability of nuclear plants and interconnectors. Over a 6-month horizon (winter 2026–27 into spring 2027), the price level is set mainly by Nordic hydrology, European gas prices and the Q1 cold-and-calm risk. These pressures now point clearly upward: the futures market prices Finland at roughly €116/MWh for Q1 2027, against €40.8/MWh realised on average in 2025.\[1\]

## TL;DR
- **Short term (days–weeks): wind dominates.** In October 2026 the daily average FI price swung from €0.82/MWh (6 Oct) to €113/MWh (13 Oct) on wind alone. Wind sets the price direction, temperature sets the size of the spikes, and nuclear or interconnector outages turn a windless day into an extreme one. Finland's own wind capacity is about 9.5 GW, and Fingrid expects about 15.2 GW of peak demand on a once-a-decade cold, calm day.
- **Medium term (~6 months): Nordic hydro deficit, expensive continental gas, and cold/calm risk.** The Nordic hydrological balance was 13 TWh below normal at end-September. Norwegian reservoirs were 69.5% full against an 84.6% median in week 43. TTF gas topped €100/MWh in late October. On 30 October, FI futures stood at about €89/MWh for December 2026, about €116/MWh for Q1 2027 and about €47/MWh for Q2 2027.
- **What you should do:** for winter 2026–27, plan around a Finnish level of roughly €90–120/MWh, with frequent near-zero windy days and spikes above €300/MWh in cold, calm weeks (February 2026 averaged about €137/MWh). Expect a sharp drop from about May 2027. That drop depends on the spring flood, wind and the OL1/OL2 spring outages; OL3 has no outage in 2027.

## Key Findings

1. **Wind has replaced hydro as the main short-term price driver in Finland.** Installed wind capacity rose to 9.44 GW at end-2025\[2\] and produced a record 22 TWh, 26.1% of demand (IEA Wind).\[3\] Prices move inversely with wind almost one-for-one. In 2025, 465 hours were negative, 40.5% of hours were above €30/MWh, and about 37% of hours were below €10/MWh.\[4\] A single 1–2 day forecast change can move the daily average by €50–100/MWh.
2. **New wind build has slowed sharply.** Fingrid's January–September 2026 review shows only 191 MW of wind connected, against 802 MW a year earlier, while solar additions rose to 562 MW from 376 MW. The extra wind dampening that helped 2024–25 is therefore not growing this winter, while e-boilers and batteries are absorbing more of the surplus hours.
3. **2026 is a high-price year because of external tightness, not Finnish fundamentals.** The drivers are a dry Nordic year, low European gas storage, the Strait of Hormuz disruption that pushed TTF above €100/MWh, and weak Swedish nuclear output.\[5\] February 2026 averaged 17.22 c/kWh incl. VAT (about €137/MWh), 190% above February 2025.\[6\] September 2026 averaged 7.15 c/kWh incl. VAT (about €57/MWh), up 37% year on year.\[7\]
4. **The Aurora Line (Nov 2025) changed the winter price structure.** Fingrid says SE1–FI price differences and volatility have already "levelled out".\[8\] The FI EPAD for Q1 2027 is about −€24/MWh against the system price, which shows Finland is expected to price well below the Nordic average. Estonia and Fenno-Skan still let Baltic and continental scarcity in during calm hours.
5. **The forward curve is the best 6-month predictor, and it is steeply seasonal.** On 30 October, FI quotes were about €89/MWh (Dec-26), €125/MWh (Jan-27), €116/MWh (Q1-27) and €47/MWh (Q2-27).\[9\] A year earlier, Q1-26 traded at about €70–72/MWh.\[5\]\[10\] The final Q1-26 outcome (Jan–Feb far above that) shows forwards can underestimate cold-and-calm risk.

## Details by factor

### 1. Wind power output and forecasts — Short 5 / Medium 3
- **Mechanism.** Wind has near-zero marginal cost. When it blows, Finnish supply exceeds demand and prices collapse to the floor set by export capacity and flexible demand (e-boilers, batteries). In calm hours, Finland relies on imports and dispatchable plants, and the price jumps to the SE3/Baltic/continental level.
- **Evidence.**
  - Daily FI averages in October 2026 included €0.82 (6 Oct), €100.31 (8 Oct), €20.25 (9 Oct), €113.13 (13 Oct), €8.11 (15 Oct) and €77.77 (18 Oct).\[11\]\[12\]
  - On 29 October, FI averaged €17.6/MWh while SE3 was at €121/MWh and DK2 at €306/MWh. The next day's FI average was €153.6/MWh.\[13\]
  - Analysts attribute the 2025 single-day high of about 38 c/kWh incl. tax to "decreased output from wind energy facilities" (Yle).\[14\]
  - In January–February 2026, Finland had its lowest wind speeds in 20 years (Elenger), which coincided with the year's highest prices.\[15\]
- **Negative prices.** There were 725 negative hours in 2024 and 465 in 2025 (Electricity Maps).\[1\] Nord Pool notes that negative prices were rarer in 2025 and in summer 2026 because of low southern Norwegian reservoirs.\[16\] Growing e-boiler and battery demand also lifts the floor.
- **Wind value erosion.** One analytics vendor estimates that wind earned only about 55% of the baseload price in 2025 (€22.4 vs €40.5/MWh).\[4\] This vendor source is lower quality, but it is consistent with the strong inverse correlation.
- **Forecast accuracy.** Day-ahead wind forecast errors move volume into intraday and balancing markets, which are now 15-minute products, and these errors drive intraday price deviations. Beyond about 7–10 days, wind is close to climatological. That is why it rates 5 at short horizons but only 3 at six months, where it matters through the expected monthly wind index (e.g. a calm February).

### 2. Temperature and weather-driven demand — Short 4 / Medium 4
- **Mechanism.** Finland has a large electric-heating load. Demand roughly doubles from summer (7–9 GW) to winter peaks (about 15 GW). Commercial sources estimate about 300 MW extra per degree of frost; treat this as indicative.\[17\]
- **Evidence.**
  - Fingrid's winter 2026–27 assessment projects 15,200 MW of demand on an extremely cold, calm day. Domestic production would cover 12,000 MW, and the remaining 3,200 MW would require imports from Sweden and Estonia.\[18\]
  - Fingrid also notes that "high electricity prices are expected to activate demand response".\[18\]
  - Fingrid's Q1 2026 review records the all-time consumption record of 15,553 MW on 8 January 2026, driven by "the cold winter weather and increase in electric district heating"; production peaked at 15,475 MW on 10 January.
  - January–February 2026 was among the coldest in 20 years in Finland and the Baltics (Elenger).\[15\] Cold plus calm produced Finland's most expensive month in years (February ~€137/MWh excl. VAT; 59 hours above 30 c/kWh).\[19\]
- **Why it rates 4 on both horizons.** In the short term, a forecast cold snap combined with low wind is the classic spike setup. At six months, the cold-winter scenario is the main upside risk to the forward price. Cold also reduces Nordic hydro inflow and increases continental gas burn.

### 3. Nuclear availability — Short 4 (event-driven) / Medium 3
- **Scale.** Nuclear supplied 37% of 2025 consumption. Olkiluoto alone covered 27.5% (TVO), and Loviisa produced 7.9 TWh (Fortum).\[20\]\[21\] OL3 (1,600 MW) is the largest single unit in the Nordic system.
- **Current status (1 Nov 2026).**
  - OL3's service outage began on 10 September. Restart was expected on 30 October but has slipped to about 7 November because of extra valve work (Yle, via Daily Northern).\[22\] Before the outage, OL3 had run at about 1,300 MW (84%) for weeks because of a reactor-coolant-pump fault (TVO, 17 Aug).\[23\]
  - Loviisa's 2026 outages (2 Aug–late Oct) are complete (Fortum, 27 Oct).\[24\]
  - A further slip at OL3 would keep November prices elevated, especially in calm periods.
- **Medium-term schedule.**
  - OL3 has moved to an 18-month cycle, so there is no OL3 outage in 2027.\[25\] The next one is spring 2028.\[26\]
  - TVO says the 2027 OL1/OL2 outages will "follow the same schedule as in 2026".\[27\] In 2026 those were OL2 on 6–16 April and OL1 on 19 April–16 June.\[25\] Expect roughly 890 MW out from April to mid-June 2027.\[28\]
  - Having no OL3 outage in spring 2027 is a bearish factor for Q2 2027 compared with 2025, when OL3 was out from March to April.
- **Rating logic.** An unplanned OL3 trip in January would remove about 10% of peak supply. Fingrid states the system can withstand "a single major failure", but prices would rise sharply.\[18\] Probability-weighted, nuclear is a 3 at six months and a 4 when an outage is actually live.

### 4. Nordic hydro: reservoirs, hydrological balance, snowpack, spring flood — Short 2 / Medium 5
- **Mechanism.** Norwegian and Swedish reservoir hydro sets the Nordic system price through its water value. Finland's own hydro (14.5% of consumption) is mostly run-of-river with limited storage.\[20\] A hydro deficit raises Swedish and Norwegian water values. This lifts SE1/SE3 prices and therefore Finland's import price and its floor in calm hours.
- **Current state.**
  - The Nordic hydrological balance was 13 TWh below normal at end-Q3 2026 (−15 TWh at end-Q2, −1 TWh a year earlier; Norsk Hydro).\[29\]
  - Norwegian reservoirs were 69.5% full in week 43 against a median of 84.6% (NVE). NO2 was at 53.6% against an 84.1% median, near a 20-year low.\[30\]
  - In February 2026, Norway's balance was −21.6 TWh, close to the record −23.2 TWh.\[31\] This came from record-low snow content rather than low reservoirs (Veyt/NVE).\[32\]
  - Vaasan Sähkö/Power-Deriva equates the about 13 TWh deficit to "the annual electricity production of... Olkiluoto 3".\[5\]
  - Julien Cossé, Chief Strategy Officer at Nord Pool, warned that "without substantial precipitation during the autumn months, reservoir levels could fall toward record lows by the end of the season and remain under pressure into 2027."
- **Spring flood.** The size of the 2027 snowpack, which accumulates from December to March, will determine the April–June price collapse. A normal or large snowpack plus wind and solar would support the low Q2-27 quote (about €47/MWh). A second low-snow year would keep the Q2 floor higher.
- **Why only 2 short-term.** Weekly precipitation forecasts move the futures curve (e.g. a "drier weather view" lifted Nordic front-quarter futures to €112.25/MWh on 28 Sep).\[33\] However, daily FI prices are dominated by wind and temperature.

### 5. Interconnectors, coupling and decoupling — Short 4 / Medium 3
- **Capacities.**
  - Aurora Line (SE1–FI, commissioned 13 Nov 2025) raised the maximum SE1–FI capacity to 1,900 MW in each direction,\[34\] adding about 800 MW for import and 900 MW for export.\[35\]
  - Fenno-Skan links Finland to SE3.
  - Estlink 1+2 link Finland to Estonia with a nominal total of 1,016 MW.\[36\]
  - There has been no Russian import since 2022, which removed a former baseload import source.
- **Evidence on coupling.**
  - Fingrid says Aurora "has levelled out price differences and the volatility of electricity prices".\[8\]
  - When Estlink 2 failed on 25 December 2024 (anchor damage, suspected Eagle S), FI–EE capacity fell from 1,016 MW to 358 MW.\[36\]\[37\] The cable was out until June 2025, repaired for €50–60 million.\[38\]
  - Ahead of winter 2025–26, Vaasan Sähkö said Aurora would "lower Finland's area price and reduce the risk of high prices in the coming winter".\[10\]
- **Short-term mechanism.** On windy days, export limits bind and Finland decouples downward, often to SE1 levels or close to zero. On calm days, Finland couples upward to SE3, Estonia or the continent. In October 2026, FI at €17.6/MWh versus DK2 at €306/MWh shows how complete the decoupling can be.\[13\] Any cable or Swedish grid outage, or reduced Aurora capacity (which depends on temperature and Swedish grid conditions), shifts these thresholds at once.\[34\]
- **Medium-term signal.** The FI EPAD (about −€24/MWh for Q1-27, about −€47/MWh for Dec-26) is the market's estimate of the expected decoupling. Swedish grid reinforcements in 2026–27 should further improve Aurora's usability (Fingrid).\[34\] Baltic-sea cable sabotage remains a tail risk.

### 6. Fuel and carbon prices (gas, coal, EUA) — Short 2 / Medium 4
- **Why they matter despite about 96% CO₂-neutral Finnish generation.** Finnish fossil generation is negligible. However, in calm, cold hours the marginal MWh is imported through Estonia/the Baltics, SE3/SE4 or the continent, where gas and coal plants set prices. High continental prices also raise Norwegian and Swedish water values through export opportunities.
- **Current levels.**
  - TTF reached about €103.6/MWh on 30 October 2026, up 233% year on year, after the Strait of Hormuz disruption.\[39\]
  - EU gas storage was about 61% in August, about 13 percentage points below the previous year (Vaasan Sähkö).\[5\]
  - EUA prices were about €88/t (12 Oct high €88.3/t; Carbon Pulse via GMK Center).\[40\]
  - Vaasan Sähkö: "High price levels in Central Europe are also reflected in the Nordic system price... affecting Finland as well, especially during periods of low wind power generation."\[5\]
  - Fuel prices alone lifted Q4 expectations by about €20/MWh between early summer and August.\[5\]
- **Rating.** Gas and carbon prices change slowly relative to daily FI price swings, but they set the level of the price during calm hours. That makes them a 4 for the six-month average.

### 7. Futures and forward market — Short 2 / Medium 4
- **Venue.** Nordic power futures (system price plus EPADs) moved from Nasdaq to Euronext Nord Pool Power Futures on 16 March 2026.\[41\] EEX also lists FI zonal futures.\[42\]\[43\]
- **FI area price quotes (system + EPAD).**
  - Latest (Euronext closes as republished by ParasSähkö, updated 31 Oct 2026): Nov-26 €90.5; Dec-26 €89.4; Jan-27 €125.2; Q1-27 €116.0; Q2-27 €47.4; Cal-27 €66.3/MWh. The system price is €139.7 for Q1-27.\[9\]
  - One week earlier (23 Oct settlement): Q1-27 €108.6.\[41\] The rise of about €8 in a week tracked gas.
- **How to use them.** Forward prices are the market's consensus on the six-month average, and they react within hours to changes in hydro, gas and nuclear schedules. They are not a forecast of individual hours.\[41\] Last winter's quote of about €70–72/MWh for Q1-26 was far exceeded in January–February 2026, so treat forwards as a central case that ignores the cold-and-calm tail.\[5\] Some retailer pages (e.g. Helen) cite materially lower Q1-27 figures,\[44\] and the reason is unclear. Rely on exchange settlement data.

### 8. Demand-side flexibility: e-boilers and demand response — Short 3 / Medium 3
- **E-boilers.** Vantaan Energia, citing Finnish Energy, reports "approximately 1,000 MW of electric boilers" in district heating at the turn of the year, "around 1,500 MW" now and "approximately 3,000 MW by the end of 2026". Examples are Helen 140 MW, Vaasa 160 MW plus 11 GWh of storage, and Vantaa 60 MW plus 700 MWh. They absorb surplus wind and lift the price floor, which is one reason negative hours fell from 725 to 465. They also free CHP and biomass during expensive hours.
- **Demand response.** Fingrid counts on price-driven demand response to protect adequacy during cold, calm peaks.\[18\] Spot-linked retail contracts and home automation mean very high quarter-hours now trigger visible load cuts.
- **Net effect.** Flexibility compresses volatility in both directions but does not change the seasonal level.

### 9. Battery storage — Short 2 (3 for intraday/15-min) / Medium 2
- Fingrid counted about 1,050 MW of storage in February 2026, roughly four times the level a year earlier (Montel). 57% of that capacity is in units larger than 10 MW.\[45\]\[46\] Large projects are coming, including OX2/Statkraft (235 MW) and Karppio (125 MW/300 MWh).\[47\]\[48\]
- Most batteries still earn money in FCR, aFRR and mFRR reserves.\[49\] Their spot effect is shaving 15-minute peaks and sawtooth patterns. With typical 1–2 hour durations, they cannot cover multi-day calm spells, so the effect on winter averages is small.

### 10. Structural demand growth (data centres, industry, hydrogen) — Short 1 / Medium 2
- Consumption was 85 TWh in 2025 (+2%; Fingrid puts it at 84.5 TWh), industrial use rose 2.6% (Finnish Energy), and Fingrid reports January–September 2026 consumption of 64.8 TWh, up 5.0%. Fingrid expects inflexible winter demand to rise by about 200 MW year on year,\[18\] and it received about 68 GW of consumption connection enquiries in the 12 months to mid-2026.\[50\]
- Most announced data-centre and hydrogen projects will not materially change demand within six months. The effect builds over years: Fingrid says consumption "could increase by as much as 40 per cent from the 2025 level", estimates Finland will need about 1,000 MW of weather-independent capacity by the turn of the decade, and warns adequacy "is at risk of deteriorating significantly" towards 2030.

### 11. CHP, district heating and condensing reserve — Short 2 / Medium 2
- CHP output follows heat demand, so it rises in cold weather and partly offsets the extra electricity demand. CHP plants increasingly switch to e-boilers when power is cheap, which reduces the electricity they supply on windy days.
- Meri-Pori (565 MW coal) leaves the NESA reserve at end-2026. It returns to the market only for January–February 2027\[18\] and then closes permanently on 1 March 2027 (Fortum).\[51\]\[52\] This adds marginal capacity for the coldest weeks, likely dispatched only at very high prices (coal plus €88/t carbon). Its closure removes a backstop for winter 2027–28.

### 12. Market structure and rules — Short 2 / Medium 1
- **15-minute day-ahead MTU** has applied since 1 October 2025 (Nord Pool/SDAC).\[53\]\[54\] It increases granularity, exposes short ramps around morning and evening peaks, and rewards batteries and flexible load. It has not shifted the overall level.\[55\]\[56\]
- **Bidding zone.** Finland is a single zone, so domestic congestion is handled through Fingrid's redispatch and its new requirements for wind- and solar-dense areas (Sept 2026), not through zonal prices.\[57\]\[58\]
- **Price limits.** The harmonised SDAC limits are −€500 and +€4,000/MWh. Finnish prices have never come close to the cap in recent years. The floor matters only on rare surplus days.
- **Other rule changes.** The Nordic flow-based capacity calculation and mFRR energy-activation market changes alter how cross-border capacity is offered to the market. Their effect shows up mostly as interconnector-driven price behaviour (factor 5).

### 13. Seasonality and time-of-day/weekday — Short 3 / Medium 4 (both predictable)
- **Seasonal.** The 2025 monthly profile ran from about €53/MWh in January to about €18/MWh in May–June, with a spike to €55/MWh in August.\[59\] In 2026, February was about €137/MWh and July was the low at 1.93 c/kWh incl. VAT.\[19\] The forward curve repeats this pattern: Q1-27 €116 versus Q2-27 €47.\[9\]
- **Intraday and weekday.** The most expensive periods are weekday 07–09 and 17–20 in cold, calm weather.\[17\] Nights and weekends are cheaper. In spring and summer, solar (402 MW added in H1 2026) increasingly pushes midday prices toward zero.\[50\]

### 14. External supply shocks (Swedish/continental nuclear, Baltic plants) — Short 3 / Medium 3
- Vaasan Sähkö reports that "annual maintenance at Sweden's nuclear power plants was delayed, and Swedish nuclear power production this summer was the lowest in fifteen years." Heatwaves forced French nuclear curtailments (Vaasan Sähkö). In Estonia, Auvere was offline from 9 January to 6 February 2026 during the cold peak.\[15\]
- These shocks act through the same coupling channels as factor 5. Watch the Nord Pool UMMs for Ringhals, Forsmark and Oskarshamn.

### 15. Taxes and consumer price (brief) — Medium 1
- Retail spot contracts pass through the FI quarter-hour price plus a margin (e.g. Helen adds 0.49 c/kWh).\[60\] Fixed contracts follow the forward curve with a risk premium.\[41\]
- The household electricity tax (class I) is 2.917875 c/kWh incl. VAT from 1 April 2026. VAT is 25.5%, which multiplies every wholesale move by 1.255 for households.\[61\]
- No tax change of a size comparable to wholesale moves is confirmed for the coming six months. The temporary 10% electricity VAT of winter 2022–23 is a precedent for intervention only in crises.\[62\]

## Notable 2025–2026 developments that changed price dynamics
- **15-minute day-ahead market:** live since 1 October 2025.\[63\]
- **Aurora Line:** commissioned 13 November 2025, six weeks early, with lower FI–SE1 spreads.\[8\]
- **Estlink 2:** sabotage-type outage from December 2024 to June 2025, then restored.\[36\]\[38\]
- **Winter 2025–26:** record-cold, low-wind January–February, with prices around €100–140/MWh.\[15\]
- **Hydro:** very dry Nordic hydro year (a record-low snowpack in February), with the southern Norway deficit continuing into autumn.\[32\]
- **Gas:** Strait of Hormuz disruption and gas storage shortfall,\[5\] with TTF above €100/MWh in late October 2026.\[39\]\[64\]
- **Storage:** quadrupling of battery capacity to about 1 GW and fast growth of e-boilers.\[46\]
- **Wind build:** slowed to 191 MW in January–September 2026.\[65\]
- **Meri-Pori:** final coal season (Jan–Feb 2027) before closure.\[18\]\[51\]
- **Futures trading:** moved to Euronext Nord Pool (March 2026).\[41\]\[42\]

## Recommendations
1. **Short-term forecasting (days–weeks):** weight inputs as wind forecast first (FMI/ECMWF ensembles), then temperature, then Nord Pool UMMs for OL3 restart, Swedish nuclear and interconnectors, then the SE3/EE/DE price level. A calm, cold weekday with any large outage is the setup for spikes of €200–500+/MWh. Windy weekends with mild weather point to near-zero or negative quarter-hours.
2. **Six-month budgeting:** use the FI forward strip (about €90/MWh for Nov–Dec, about €116/MWh for Q1, about €47/MWh for Q2) as the base case. Add an upside scenario for Q1 of about €140+/MWh, consistent with February 2026, for a cold, calm winter combined with gas above €100/MWh and a worsening hydro deficit.
3. **Hedging:** if you are exposed to Q1 2027, the asymmetry favours hedging at least part of January–February. Last year's forwards underestimated the outcome, and the hydro and gas setup is tighter now. Q2 2027 carries less risk: there is no OL3 outage, and the spring flood is the main swing factor.
4. **Indicators to monitor weekly:** the Nordic hydrological balance (NVE, Montel), TTF and EU gas storage, the FI EPAD, OL3's actual restart, Aurora and Estlink available transfer capacity, and the 2-week temperature and wind outlook.

## Caveats
- The October 2026 FI monthly average (about €38/MWh) and some daily figures come from consumer price aggregators that republish Nord Pool/ENTSO-E data.\[66\] They are not official Nord Pool statistics, and aggregator figures disagree slightly.
- The latest forward quotes are Euronext closes republished by Finnish comparison sites. Retailer-cited forwards (e.g. Helen) differ materially.
- The impact ratings are analytical judgements based on the evidence above, not outputs of an econometric model. The per-degree demand sensitivity and the wind capture-rate figures come from commercial sources.
- Some forward-looking statements in the sources (e.g. "reservoirs could fall toward record lows", Swedish grid improvements in 2026–27) are projections, not outcomes.
- No official Nordic hydrological balance figure for mid/late October was found. The −13 TWh figure is as of 30 September.
